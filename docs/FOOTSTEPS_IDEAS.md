how do we let npcs track footsteps even when the player zig-zags quickly

The trick stealth games use is not actually tracking footprints one-by-one. That becomes expensive and unreliable when a player zig-zags quickly.

Instead they create a temporary “sound scent field” on the grid that NPCs follow like a gradient. This works extremely well for tile engines and roguelikes and costs almost nothing.

Games that use variations of this include Metal Gear Solid, Invisible, Inc., and many procedural stealth roguelikes.

1. Convert footsteps into a noise map

Instead of NPCs chasing footprints directly, every step deposits noise energy into the tile grid.

noiseGrid[x][y] += loudness

Example values:

walk  = 4
run   = 7
sprint = 10

This creates a temporary gradient around the player’s path.

2. Noise diffuses slightly

Each frame, noise spreads to neighboring tiles.

Very cheap rule:

tileNoise = max(
  self,
  north - 1,
  south - 1,
  east - 1,
  west - 1
)

So the sound spreads like this:

player step
     10
   9 10 9
  8  9  8

NPCs will naturally move toward higher numbers.

This is why zig-zagging still works — the gradient stays smooth.

3. Noise decays over time

Every tick:

noiseGrid[x][y] -= decay

Example:

decay = 0.5 per frame

So footsteps fade naturally.

After ~3 seconds they disappear.

4. NPCs follow the gradient

When an NPC hears something, it switches to investigate mode.

Movement rule:

look at 8 neighboring tiles
move to the tile with highest noise

Example:

 2 3 4
 3 5 6   ← NPC moves toward 6
 2 4 7

This creates the illusion that the NPC is tracking the path.

5. Why zig-zagging still works

Player path:

P
 ↘
  ↙
   ↘

Footprints would look like a messy zig-zag.

But the noise field becomes a smooth trail:

   4
  5 6
 7 8 9

NPCs follow the gradient instead of exact steps.

So they curve naturally along the path.

6. Cheap directional hint (optional)

Each footstep can also store movement direction.

noiseDir[x][y] = direction

When an NPC reaches the tile, it biases movement slightly in that direction.

Example:

preferredDir = noiseDir[tile]

This makes tracking much more convincing.

But it’s optional.

7. Suspicion zones

NPCs shouldn’t chase every noise.

Add a threshold:

if noise > hearingThreshold
    investigate

Example:

threshold = 3

Quiet footsteps might only trigger nearby enemies.

8. Walls block sound cheaply

When propagating noise:

if tile is wall:
   stop propagation

So sound flows around rooms naturally.

This works perfectly with ASCII grids.

9. Performance cost

For a typical map (80×80 tiles):

~6400 cells
simple math

This is extremely cheap even on mobile.

And you can update it every 2–3 frames instead of every frame.

10. Visual debugging (very helpful)

During development, render the noise field as numbers or colors.

Example:

0 0 1 2
1 3 5 6
0 2 4 7

You’ll immediately see if NPC tracking works.

If you'd like, I can also show you a very clever trick used in stealth roguelikes that lets enemies predict where the player is heading (not just where they were) — which makes guards feel dramatically smarter without any heavy AI computation.

how do enemies predict where the player is heading

You can make enemies predict player movement without heavy AI by extending the same noise / footstep system you already built. The trick is to treat footsteps not just as where the player was, but also as a vector indicating momentum.

Instead of NPCs only following the loudest tile, they bias their movement slightly ahead of the trail.

This gives the illusion of smart guards while staying extremely cheap.

1. Store direction with each footstep

Every step already has a direction (your 8-direction combat logic). Save that with the noise.

noiseGrid[x][y]
noiseDir[x][y]  // 0..7 direction

Example direction encoding:

0 N
1 NE
2 E
3 SE
4 S
5 SW
6 W
7 NW

So when a step lands:

noiseGrid[x][y] += loudness
noiseDir[x][y] = direction

This costs one extra integer per tile.

2. Predict forward tiles

When an NPC reaches a tile with noise, instead of stopping there they look ahead in the stored direction.

Example prediction:

futureTile1 = tile + directionVector
futureTile2 = tile + directionVector * 2

These tiles get a temporary prediction score.

predictionScore = noiseValue * 0.6

Now the guard tends to move ahead of the trail instead of always behind it.

3. Combine prediction with gradient tracking

NPC movement decision becomes:

score = noiseGrid + predictionScore

Then they move toward the highest score.

Example grid:

2 3 4
3 5 6
2 4 7

Prediction might produce:

2 3 4
3 5 6
2 4 7
      6  ← predicted tile

The NPC moves toward the future location, not the last footprint.

4. Momentum smoothing

Players often zig-zag. If NPCs reacted instantly to every direction change they'd jitter.

So average the last few directions.

Store:

lastDir
currentDir

Blend them:

predictedDir = lerp(lastDir, currentDir, 0.5)

This produces a smooth pursuit arc instead of jagged turns.

5. Limit prediction distance

Too much prediction feels unfair.

Typical stealth games limit prediction to 1–3 tiles.

Example:

walk prediction = 1 tile
run prediction = 2 tiles
sprint prediction = 3 tiles

Fast movement leaves a longer predictive trail.

6. Suspicion affects prediction strength

Low suspicion → NPCs follow the trail normally.

High suspicion → NPCs predict aggressively.

Example:

curious        prediction = 0.2
investigate    prediction = 0.5
alert          prediction = 0.8

This makes guards feel smarter when alarmed.

7. Handle sharp turns

If the player suddenly changes direction more than ~90°:

angleDifference > 90°

Reduce prediction temporarily.

prediction *= 0.5

This prevents guards from overshooting when the player jukes.

8. Cheap interception behavior

Occasionally allow NPCs to cut corners.

Rule:

if npc is close to predicted path
    move diagonally across trail

This creates moments where guards appear to anticipate the escape route.

9. Works perfectly with your 8-direction system

Because your combat and movement already use 8 directions, prediction math stays simple.

Direction vectors:

N  (0,-1)
NE (1,-1)
E  (1,0)
SE (1,1)
S  (0,1)
SW (-1,1)
W  (-1,0)
NW (-1,-1)

No trig required.

10. Final NPC decision rule

Each step:

candidateScore =
    noiseGrid
  + predictionField
  - distancePenalty

Move to the neighbor with the highest score.

This produces behavior that feels like:

guards follow footsteps
guards anticipate direction
guards cut off escape

All with extremely simple math.

Resulting gameplay

Walking player:

NPC slowly tracks trail

Running player:

NPC moves ahead of the trail

Player zig-zags:

NPC follows curved path but sometimes overshoots

Hiding:

noise fades
prediction collapses
NPC loses target

This creates convincing stealth pursuit while remaining very cheap for mobile browsers and DOM-based engines.
